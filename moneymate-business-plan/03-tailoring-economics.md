# 03 · Tailoring Economics — Pricing, Capacity, Margin

All figures are **Jalandhar / Doaba planning assumptions for 2026-27**, built from published
Indian boutique pricing benchmarks and Punjab wage/rent levels. Calibrate against your own
market by visiting 5 competing shops with a notebook before you print your rate card.

---

## 1. The core problem, stated honestly

A tailoring shop's revenue = `tailors × units/day × charge/unit × days`.

Every one of those four variables is capped:
- **tailors** — capped by floor space and your ability to pay salaries
- **units/day** — capped by skill (a good suit takes 12–20 hours; a blouse 2–4)
- **charge/unit** — capped by what the shop next door charges
- **days** — capped by the calendar and by seasonality

The model in `financial-model.md` shows what this means in practice for the *same* 3-tailor shop:

| Model | Units/mo | Charge/unit | Revenue/mo | EBITDA/mo | EBITDA % |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| A1 volume alteration shop | 234 | ₹420 | ₹0.98 L | **–₹0.34 L** | –35% |
| A2 typical mid custom tailor | 172 | ₹950 | ₹1.63 L | **₹0.12 L** | 7% |
| A3 premium made-to-measure studio | 109 | ₹2,600 | ₹2.84 L | **₹0.73 L** | 26% |
| A4 = A3 with 6 tailors + fabric attach | 234 | ₹2,600 | ₹6.08 L | **₹2.98 L** | 49% |

**A3 stitches 37% fewer garments than A1 and earns ₹1.07 L more per month.**

That is the entire game: **the charge per unit, not the number of units.**

Three ways to raise the charge per unit without raising your skill:
1. **Include the cloth** (fabric margin rides on the same bill) → this is your Rung 4.
2. **Include the service** (2–3 fittings, home measurement, delivery, alteration guarantee,
   garment bag) → this is your Rung 2–3.
3. **Include the occasion** (a groom's sherwani is not priced like a Tuesday shirt) → this is
   positioning and seasonality.

| Lehenga | ₹800–1,800 | ₹1,200–2,500 | ₹1,500–3,000 |
| Shirt | ₹350–500 | ₹500–900 | ₹700–1,500 |
| Trouser | ₹400–600 | ₹600–1,000 | ₹900–1,800 |
| 2-piece suit (stitching only) | ₹1,800–2,800 | ₹2,800–5,000 | ₹4,000–9,000 |
| Sherwani | ₹2,500–5,000 | ₹5,000–12,000 | ₹10,000–30,000 |

Typical published margin structure: **alterations 60–80%**, basic stitching 40–50%,
designer wear 30–40%, bridal 35–45%. Net profit for a well-run shop 30–50%; home tailors
earn ₹15,000–30,000/month, small shops ₹30,000–1,00,000/month, niche premium tailors ₹2 L+.

**Where you should sit:** top of the tier-2 band, at 80–90% of metro pricing. Jalandhar has
metro purchasing power in specific pockets (Model Town, Civil Lines, Urban Estate, and the NRI
families) and tier-3 purchasing power everywhere else. **Price for the pocket, not the city.**

## 4. The Cloth + Stitch combo — pricing worked example

Take a mid poly-viscose suiting: **landed ₹230/m**, wholesale-equivalent ₹285/m, retail ₹399/m.

| Way you sell it | Customer pays | Your cost | Gross profit | GP % |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Stitching only (customer's cloth), 2-piece suit | ₹3,500 | labour+consumables ~₹1,700 | ₹1,800 | 51% |
| **Cloth only**, 3.5 m @ ₹399 | ₹1,397 | ₹805 | ₹592 | 42% |
| **Combo**: 3.5 m cloth + lining + buttons + full stitching, quoted as one price | **₹6,500** | cloth ₹805 + lining/trims ₹350 + labour ₹1,700 = ₹2,855 | **₹3,645** | **56%** |
| Combo, "discounted" version (cloth at ₹349/m) | ₹5,900 | ₹2,700 | ₹3,200 | 54% |

Read that again: **the combo earns ₹3,645 vs ₹1,800 for stitching-only — twice the profit from the
same customer visit, the same tailor hours and the same machine.** And you can even *discount* it
(₹5,900) and still out-earn the full-price stitching-only order.

**Discounting from a bundle is always safer than discounting a service.** That's the trick.

> the customer's confusion, raises your ticket, and moves three slow items with one fast one.

## 5. Capacity — how many orders can you actually take?

**Hours-based capacity model.** Assume 9 productive machine-hours per tailor per day, 26 days.

| Garment | Tailor hours | Orders/tailor/month |
|---|---|---|
| Shirt | 3.0 | 78 |
| Trouser | 3.5 | 67 |
| Kurta-pyjama | 6.0 | 39 |
| Blazer | 14 | 17 |
| 2-piece suit (half canvas) | 22 | 10 |
| Sherwani | 30 | 7 |
| Blouse | 2.5 | 93 |
| Lehenga | 18 | 12 |

A blended realistic load is **~2.2 "units"/tailor/day** for volume work and **~1.4–1.6/day**
for premium work. Note the trap: **premium work uses more hours but earns 5–10× more per hour.**

| | Shirt | 2-piece suit (Essential) | 2-piece suit (Bespoke combo) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Revenue | ₹600 | ₹3,500 | ₹14,000 |
| Tailor hours | 3 | 22 | 24 |
| **Revenue per tailor hour** | **₹200** | **₹159** | **₹583** |

**Conclusion:** a shop full of shirt orders is a poor shop. A shop full of bespoke combos is a
rich shop. Your front desk should be trained to *steer* customers up, and your cutting schedule
should protect hours for high-value work in season.

**Rule for accepting orders:** never book more than **110% of capacity**. The 10% is your rework
buffer. At 130% you start delivering late, and late delivery in wedding season is a
reputation death.

## 6. Cost structure of a 3-tailor studio (monthly)

| Line | Amount | % of revenue |
|---|---|---|
| Revenue (A2 scenario) | ₹1,63,000 | 100% |
| Consumables (thread, buttons, zips, interlining, paper, blades) | ₹6,500 | 4% |
| Tailors (3 × ₹18,000) | ₹54,000 | 33% |
| Master cutter | ₹26,000 | 16% |
| Helper/finisher | ₹9,000 | 6% |
| Rent | ₹32,000 | 20% |
| Electricity | ₹6,500 | 4% |
| Marketing | ₹8,150 | 5% |
| Misc (software, repairs, accountant, tea) | ₹9,000 | 6% |
| **EBITDA** | **₹11,850** | **7%** |

**Break-even = 158 units/month = 6.1 units/day.** Memorise that number.

Where the money leaks, in order of size:
1. **Rent paid for space you don't use** — a 600 sq ft shop with 3 machines is a disaster.
2. **Idle tailor hours** — a tailor at ₹18,000 costs ₹77/hour whether or not he stitches.
3. **Rework** — every redo is a free garment plus an angry customer.
4. **Fabric wastage** (once you stock cloth) — bad cutting, end-pieces, shop damage, theft.
5. **Unbilled work** — "chhota alteration hai, rehne do". At ₹200 × 3/day that's ₹78,000/year.
6. **Credit given to walk-in retail customers** — never. Only advance or COD.

## 7. Cash-flow rules (tailoring is a cash business — protect that)

1. **50% advance on every order above ₹2,000.** No advance = no slot. Say it politely and firmly:
   "*Sir ji, advance de naal hi booking confirm hundi hai — assi cloth order karde haan.*"
2. **Balance on delivery, before the garment leaves the shop.** Not after. Not next week.
3. **Never give credit to a retail customer.** Credit is only for verified trade buyers with a
   written limit (see `07`).
4. **Keep 2 months of fixed cost as cash.** In a seasonal business, October–December pays for
   July–September.
5. **Fabric is bought on credit from suppliers only when you can pay within the discount window.**
   Ludhiana/Delhi wholesalers often give 2–3% for cash-in-7-days; that discount is pure margin.
6. **Take UPI, card and a QR at the counter.** Every "cash nahi hai, kal de jaanga" is a lost sale.

## 8. The annual price rise ritual (do this every October)

1. **1 October** — recalculate your cost per tailor-hour from the last 12 months of books.
2. **5 October** — set new prices: `cost per hour × target hours per garment × (1 + overhead%) × (1 + margin%)`.
3. **10 October** — print the new rate card, dated, with the old one filed.
4. **15 October** — WhatsApp the entire customer list: *"1 November ton navi rate list lagu ho rahi
   hai. Purane customers layi 31 October tak purani rate — eh season di booking layi best time hai."*
5. **Result:** a 10% price rise *and* a pre-season booking surge, in the same message.

Never raise prices silently. A dated announcement makes the increase feel like a policy, not a
personal insult.
